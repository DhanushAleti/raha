# Sales playbook — scripts you can read out loud

For calls, DMs, and email. Written to be used verbatim on your first ten calls, then adapted.

---

## 1. The decision you asked me to make: what do you send them?

**You send the free audit. Always. `https://rahatax.vercel.app/audit`**

Not the landing page. Not a waitlist. Here's why, and this is not close:

| Option | What it asks | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Landing page | "Give me ₹20,000" | Too big an ask for someone who has never heard of you. Cold traffic doesn't buy a ₹20,000 annual plan off one email. |
| Waitlist | "Wait for something that doesn't exist yet" | **Actively wrong.** Waitlists are for unbuilt products. Yours is live. Putting a working product behind a waitlist throws away your only advantage and creates zero urgency. |
| **Free audit** | "Answer 8 questions, 2 minutes" | **This one.** Free, fast, and it ends with *them* looking at a red score about *their own money*. They diagnose themselves. You get their email and their score. |

The audit is the whole strategy. A stranger will not buy from you, but they will spend two
minutes checking whether they have a problem. When the result says RED, the conversation stops
being "why should I trust you" and becomes "how bad is this." That's the only conversion that matters.

**The funnel:** DM or email → audit link → red/amber score → they book a call → you close.

Never send the pricing page before the audit. Never.

### Sell the ₹2,000 diagnostic first, not the ₹20,000 seat

Until somebody has paid you anything, ₹20,000 is not a price — it's a hypothesis. Lead with this:

> "Before you commit to anything annual — let me do this properly for one financial year. ₹2,000,
> I go through your actual payouts and bank statements, tell you exactly which FIRCs are missing
> and what your real exposure is, in writing. If it comes back clean, you've spent ₹2,000 to stop
> worrying. If it's bad, you'll know the number before the department does, and we can talk about
> the annual plan then."

Why this is the better first ask:
- **₹2,000 is a decision, ₹20,000 is a deliberation.** One happens on the call; the other happens "next week" and then never.
- **It needs no product.** You do it by hand in an evening. You are the product.
- **Money is the only real signal.** "Sounds useful" is worth nothing. Someone paying ₹2,000 to a
  stranger for a tax diagnostic tells you more than a hundred waitlist signups.
- **It's the strongest possible lead-in to the annual plan** — you'll have seen their books.

If nobody will pay ₹2,000, nobody was ever going to pay ₹20,000. Better to learn that this week
than after three months of outreach.

## 2. Ammunition — memorise these, they are your entire pitch

All of this is real and checkable. Say the numbers with certainty; that's where conviction comes from.

- GST registration is triggered at **₹20 lakh aggregate turnover** — that's AdSense + brand deals + memberships + super chats + merch, combined. Most creators only count brand deals.
- AdSense from Google is a **zero-rated export of service** — 0% GST — **but only if you can prove it**. Proof is the FIRC (Foreign Inward Remittance Certificate) from your bank.
- **Without the FIRC, the GST department treats your Google money as domestic income and charges 18% on it.**
- FIRCs should be collected **within 15 days** of each foreign receipt. Almost nobody does this. Banks get slow and unhelpful about old ones.
- You also need an **LUT (Form RFD-11), filed fresh every financial year**, to export without paying IGST up front.
- Penalty for unregistered taxable turnover: **100% of the unpaid tax, plus 18% per year interest** — and it compounds across every year you were unregistered, up to three.

### The scare math — run this live on a call

> "You said roughly ₹60 lakh last year, about ₹40 lakh of that from AdSense. If those FIRCs
> aren't there, that ₹40 lakh gets read as domestic. Eighteen percent is ₹7.2 lakh. Penalty is
> another ₹7.2 lakh. Interest at eighteen percent over two years is about ₹2.6 lakh. So you're
> looking at roughly ₹17 lakh of exposure sitting on a thing you thought was handled.
> That's an estimate — your CA has to confirm it. But the shape of it doesn't change."

Then, quietly: **"Raha is ₹20,000 a year."**

Let the silence sit. Do not fill it. That ratio is the close, and it argues for itself.

## 3. Cold DM — Instagram / X / LinkedIn (best channel for creators)

Creators live in DMs and ignore email. Keep it short enough to read in the notification preview.

**Message 1 — the hook**

> Hey [name] — quick one, not a pitch. Do you collect FIRCs from your bank for your AdSense
> payouts? Most creators don't, and it's the thing that turns 0% GST into 18%. Built a free
> 2-min check that tells you where you stand: rahatax.vercel.app/audit

**If they reply "what's a FIRC?"** — that's a buying signal, not ignorance. Answer plainly:

> It's the certificate from your bank proving the money came from abroad. It's what makes your
> AdSense a zero-rated export instead of taxable domestic income. Your bank has them; they just
> don't hand them over unless you ask. If you've never asked, the audit will come back red —
> worth the 2 minutes.

**Message 2 — day 4, no reply**

> No worries if this isn't a priority — last thing from me. The one to check is whether your LUT
> is filed for this financial year. It expires annually and almost every creator I talk to has
> let it lapse without knowing. Takes 30 seconds to check with your CA.

That second message closes more than the first, because it gives value and asks for nothing.

## 5. The discovery call — read this top to bottom

Twenty minutes. You are not "having a chat." You are running a diagnosis. The structure below is
the Straight Line — open, gather, present, close, loop — adapted for a compliance product.

One adaptation that matters: **trust is the product here.** Boiler-room pressure sells a
commodity; it destroys a tax service, because the buyer is already anxious and one bad referral
travels fast in a community this small. So you get the structure, the certainty, and the urgency —
but every claim stays true. The fear does the selling. You don't have to.

### Open — 30 seconds

> "Hi [name], it's Sunny from Raha. You ran the compliance check and came back red on the FIRC
> and LUT sections — that's why I wanted fifteen minutes. Is now still good?"

*Tonality: brisk, warm, slightly clinical. You are a doctor with their X-ray, not a salesman.
The fact that they ran the audit means you were invited. Act like it.*

### Gather — 5 minutes. Ask, then shut up.

1. "Roughly what did you earn across everything last financial year?"
2. "How much of that came from abroad — AdSense, Patreon, foreign brands?"
3. "Are you GST registered?"
4. "Who handles your filing right now?"
5. "Has your CA ever asked your bank for a FIRC?" ← **the money question**
6. "Do you know if your LUT is filed for this year?"

Question 5 is where the call turns. The answer is almost always *no* or *I don't know*. Do not
gloat. Say: **"Okay. That's normal, and it's fixable. Let me show you what it means."**

*Never make them feel stupid. Shame makes people leave the call and do nothing. They must feel
that a system failed them, not that they failed.*

### Present — 3 minutes, three certainties

**Certainty 1 — the problem is real and theirs.** Run the scare math out loud with *their* numbers
from the gather. Specific beats general every time.

**Certainty 2 — you are the person who fixes it.**
> "I only do creators. Not businesses, not salaried people — creators with foreign income. That's
> the entire product. Your CA is good at what they do; they've just never had a client whose money
> arrives from Singapore every month."

**Certainty 3 — the mechanism is concrete.**
> "Raha tracks every payout, matches it to the FIRC from your bank, flags what's unmatched before
> it becomes exposure, generates GST-correct invoices, and tells you what to set aside each month.
> A chartered accountant reviews and signs off on the filings. I don't file anything myself —
> a qualified CA does that, and every number Raha shows is an estimate until they confirm it."

*Say that last sentence exactly. Never claim you file returns. It's not true, it's not legal for
you to imply, and if a customer repeats it to their CA you lose all credibility in one sentence.*

### Close — ask once, cleanly

> "Founding Creator is ₹20,000 for the year. Ten seats, and I'm keeping it at ten so I can actually
> sit with each person and get their FIRC backlog cleaned up personally. After that it goes up and
> becomes self-serve. Do you want one of the seats?"

Then **stop talking.** Whoever speaks first, loses. Count to ten in your head if you have to.

## 6. Objections — answer, re-certify, close again

Never answer an objection and then wait. Answer it, rebuild one certainty, ask for the sale again.
That loop is the single highest-leverage thing in this document.

**"I already have a CA."**
> "Good — keep them. I'm not replacing your CA, I'm giving them the paperwork they've never had.
> Ask them one question tonight: 'have you collected my FIRCs?' If they say yes, you don't need
> me and I'll tell you so myself. If they go quiet, that's the gap. Shall I hold a seat while you
> ask them?"

**"₹20,000 is a lot right now."**
> "It is real money. Set it against the ₹17 lakh we just worked out — that's about one percent.
> And it's not a subscription that quietly renews; it's the year where the backlog gets cleaned
> up, which is the expensive part. Is the hesitation the amount, or is it that you're not yet sure
> this problem is actually yours?"

*That question is the important half. It forces the real objection out. Price is almost never the
real objection.*

**"Let me think about it."**
> "Completely fair. Can I ask what specifically you want to think about — whether the exposure is
> real, or whether I'm the right person to fix it?"

Then handle whichever one they name, and close again. "Let me think about it" is never the
objection; it's the wrapper around one.

**"Send me some details."**
> "I'll send it right after this. One thing though — the details won't tell you anything the audit
> didn't. What'll actually decide this is whether your FIRCs exist. Could you ask your bank or CA
> this week, and I'll call you Friday once you know? If they're all there, I'll be the first to
> tell you not to buy."

*Offering to disqualify them is the most persuasive thing you can do in a trust sale.*

**"I'll deal with it after this financial year."**
> "That's the one I'd push back on. Interest accrues monthly, and FIRCs get harder to obtain the
> older the transaction is — banks are quick about last month and very slow about eighteen months
> ago. Waiting doesn't hold the problem still, it grows it. Start now, file calmly later."

**"Why wouldn't I just use Skydo / Karbon / Xflow? They give me FIRA automatically."**

This is the objection that can end the call, and it is a fair one. Know it cold.

> "You should use them, honestly — if your money can route through them, that's the cleanest way
> to get FIRA going forward. But three things they don't do. One, they only cover money that flows
> through *them*, from the day you switch. Every rupee that already landed in your bank over the
> last two or three years has no FIRA, and that backlog is where your exposure actually sits —
> a payment rail can't fix history. Two, they're payment companies, not tax software: they don't
> compute your GST, generate compliant invoices, track whether your LUT is filed, or tell you what
> to set aside. Three, they only see foreign money. Your brand deals in rupees are 18% domestic GST
> with CGST/SGST or IGST depending on the state, and that's over half your liability.
> Use them for the pipe. Use me for the compliance."

*If they haven't switched to a payment rail yet, the backlog argument is your strongest card —
it's the part nobody else can sell them out of. If they already use Karbon and have clean FIRA
going forward and no backlog, **tell them they don't need you for FIRC** and sell on GST/invoicing
or not at all. Overselling into that is how you get a refund and a bad story.*

**"How do I know you're legit? You're new."**
> "You don't yet, and you shouldn't take my word for it. That's exactly why the first ten are
> ₹20,000 and heavily hands-on — I need ten creators who'll vouch for me more than I need the
> money. A CA reviews every filing. And you can check every number Raha gives you against your own
> bank statements. Don't trust me — verify the first month, then decide."

## 7. Hard rules — breaking these costs you the company

1. **Never say you file returns.** Raha organises; a qualified CA files. Say it that way every time.
2. **Every number is an estimate until a CA confirms it.** Say the word "estimate" out loud on calls.
3. **Never guarantee a refund, a saving, or an outcome.** "Here is your exposure" is fine.
   "You'll save ₹7 lakh" is a promise you cannot keep and may have to answer for.
4. **Never imply you're a CA or a CA firm.**
5. **If someone's audit comes back green, tell them they don't need you.** You will get more
   referrals from the people you turn away than from the ones you push.
6. **Don't oversell the Creator Economy Bill.** Use it as timing, not as a threat you've invented.

## 8. What to do the moment someone says yes

1. Send `founding-customer-closing-doc.md` within the hour, while they're still warm.
2. Collect payment before onboarding. A verbal yes is not a customer.
3. Tick the seat counter in Notion — and update the number on the site. The counter only creates
   urgency if it's honest, and if you fake it once you'll never trust your own funnel again.
4. Book their onboarding call within 72 hours. Fast delivery on customer #1 is worth more than
   customer #2.
